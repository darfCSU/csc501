Working for the government for decades I have EXTENSIVE experience dealing with poor leadership.  However, for this assignment we'll keep things positive and use my ongoing fictional class project example.  If Schumann Hudson Acquisition & Technology (SHAT) were brought into a project where the existing team had poor leadership and was consistently missing goals, I would not immediately solve the problem by adding more people/resources. Throwing more money at a problem can make a poorly managed project more complicated if responsibilities and priorities are still unclear.  Besides, my fictional company can't simply print more money.

My first step would be to assess the current workload, skills, responsibilities, and project priorities. From there, I would establish clear ownership for each major deliverable and make sure the team understands who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed.  For example, on our Darfcloud modernization project, we recently completed a sprint to backport authentication to the legacy Frosmear application. The work initially looked like a relatively small development task. Once we worked through it, however, dependencies involving existing accounts, permissions, database access, and the eventual migration of users became apparent. That experience would influence how I approach resource allocation on the next stage of the project. 

I would change the budget to reflect the team's actual ability to deliver the project rather than simply assuming that the original staffing plan is adequate. A team that repeatedly misses targets may require additional project management, technical leadership, training, or specialized expertise. Those resources would increase the budget, but the cost should be compared with the potential cost of continued delays, rework, and missed deliverables. 

External resources would be appropriate when the organization lacks a particular skill or needs temporary capacity. For example, SHAT might recommend an outside network specialist (my buddy Doug), security consultant (ChatGPT), or experienced project manager (Glenda) if those capabilities are not available internally. I would favor internal resources when the problem is primarily organizational knowledge, because internal staff already understand the systems and processes. Randomly hiring contractors leads to bankrupcy in the real world.

Most importantly, I would treat the leadership problem itself as a project risk. Adding resources without addressing unclear priorities or ineffective decision-making could simply increase the number of people waiting for direction. Establishing clear responsibilities, measurable goals, regular status reviews, and an escalation process would provide a stronger foundation. Once those controls are in place, additional resources can be used strategically to address genuine capacity or skill gaps rather than masking the underlying management problem.
