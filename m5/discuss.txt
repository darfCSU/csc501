If only 10% of the code passes basic QA, I think we have a bigger problem than bad code. We have a process problem. As project manager, I would tell the team and leadership what the results mean, identify the biggest failure patterns, and temporarily prioritize fixing defects over adding features. Blaming developers won't fix the process, and in my fictional example I can't fire them because I only have one.

This reminds me of the Joel Spolsky Test, especially his questions about daily builds, tracking bugs, and fixing bugs before writing new code. His point was that good software teams need basic engineering practices to consistently deliver quality software (Spolsky, 2000). Darfcloud is a good example of a low Joel-test scoring company.

Some Frosmear links end up in random places or on undefined pages because of Nginx routing and outdated code references. Testing a page by itself won't necessarily catch that; we need to test how the pieces work together, and invest time into proper unit and integration tests.

I'd get development and QA together, figure out what's failing, and fix the biggest problems first. QA should be involved before coding starts, not just at the end. We'd agree on what each feature needs to do, run automated tests as code changes, and require passing tests and code reviews before merging. For Darfcloud, that means testing URLs, Nginx routes, authentication, and legacy links before changes reach production (aka the internet-facing laptop). I'd keep leadership updated on whether the QA results are improving and make sure developers know what needs fixing. Chapters 10 and 11 of our textbook emphasize managing team responsibilities and communicating effectively, both of which matter here. Nobody benefits from hiding problems, so I'd rather have a team that catches and reports mistakes early.

The goal isn't to slow development down with more red tape. It's to catch problems earlier, when they're easier to fix, instead of shipping broken features and paying for the same mistakes later. Quality needs to become everyone's responsibility, not something the QA team is expected to fix at the end.
